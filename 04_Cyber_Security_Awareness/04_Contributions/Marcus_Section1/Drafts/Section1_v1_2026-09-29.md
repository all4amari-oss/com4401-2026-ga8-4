# 1. Password Hygiene

*Section owner: Marcus Weeks*

Almost every online account starts with a password. That also makes passwords one of the easiest ways in for an attacker. Verizon's 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report found that stolen or compromised login details were the starting point in 22% of the breaches it studied, more than any other method (Langlois, 2025). Password hygiene means the everyday habits that stop that from happening: how a password is made, where it is kept, and whether it gets used more than once.

## 1.1 What makes a password strong

For years the advice was to make passwords complicated, with capital letters, numbers and symbols, and to change them every few months. That advice backfired. Most people have dozens of accounts and cannot remember dozens of complicated passwords, so they fall back on predictable tricks, like swapping the letter "o" for a zero or adding "!" to the end. Attackers know these tricks and build them into their guessing tools, so forcing complexity ends up producing passwords that are more predictable, not less (National Cyber Security Centre, 2021).

The advice has now changed on both sides of the Atlantic. In the UK, the National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) recommends joining three random words together. That gives a password that is long enough and strong enough for most accounts, and much easier to remember. The NCSC also says writing a password down is fine, as long as it is kept somewhere safe (National Cyber Security Centre, n.d.a).

In the US, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) has gone further. Its latest guidance says services must require at least 15 characters for a password used on its own. It also says they must not force a mix of character types, and must not make people change passwords on a schedule unless there is evidence the password has been leaked. Services should instead check new passwords against lists of common and already-leaked ones (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2025).

The research backs this up. A study of 15 different password rules with over 20,000 participants found that rules based on length can produce passwords that are both stronger and easier to use than rules based on complexity (Shay et al., 2016). The NCSC makes the same point about length. A password made of several words is naturally longer, without relying on predictable add-ons like an "!" at the end (National Cyber Security Centre, 2021).

## 1.2 Password managers

Even with three random words, nobody can remember a different password for every account. A password manager solves this. You log in once with one strong main password, and the manager stores the rest, creates new random passwords and fills them in for you (National Cyber Security Centre, n.d.b).

The benefits are practical. Every account gets its own password, so one leak does not open the others. Autofill only works on the genuine website, which gives some protection against fake login pages. Many managers also warn you if one of your passwords turns up in a data breach (National Cyber Security Centre, n.d.b). NIST now says websites must allow password managers and autofill, because they make people more likely to choose stronger passwords (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2025).

They are not perfect. A security test of 13 popular password managers found that some generated passwords were not truly random, and found insecure default settings and other weaknesses. The same study found they had improved a lot over the previous five years (Oesch and Ruoti, 2020). How people use them matters too. Interviews with 30 users found that people using the password manager built into their browser were mostly after convenience, while people who installed a separate one were more focused on security. Earlier data suggested the convenience group more often kept weak or reused passwords (Pearman et al., 2019).

Even with those weaknesses, the NCSC still recommends password managers. Its advice is to turn on 2-step verification first, as the most important step, then use a password manager on top of it (National Cyber Security Centre, n.d.b).

## 1.3 Credential reuse and credential stuffing

Credential reuse means using the same password on more than one account. It is common. A study of leaked passwords from 11 websites estimated that 43% to 51% of users reuse the same password across sites. People who change a password slightly between sites were not much safer. Using a known password from one site, the researchers' method guessed 30% of these tweaked passwords within 100 attempts (Das et al., 2014). Verizon's more recent data points the same way. In the median case, only 49% of a person's passwords were different from each other (Langlois, 2025).

Reuse is what makes credential stuffing work. Attackers take usernames and passwords leaked in one breach and use software to try them on thousands of other sites. They do not need to guess anything. They just need people to have reused a password. Verizon found that credential stuffing made up a median of 19% of all daily login attempts at the organisations it studied, rising to 25% at large companies (Langlois, 2025).

The 23andMe breach shows what this looks like in real life. Between April and September 2023, an attacker used login details stolen from other, unrelated breaches to get into accounts on the genetic testing site. The personal data of 155,592 people in the UK was exposed, including names, locations, ethnicity, family trees and health reports. In June 2025 the UK Information Commissioner's Office fined 23andMe £2.31 million for failing to protect that data (Information Commissioner's Office, 2025).

## 1.4 Where the numbers come from

The 22% figure at the start of this section comes from Verizon's 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report. It is based on the known ways attackers got in across 9,891 breaches (Langlois, 2025). It is quoted here from Verizon's own research team, not from a news article repeating it.

## 1.5 Password hygiene at a glance

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Use three random words, or at least 15 characters | Rely on symbols and number swaps like "P@ssw0rd" |
| Use a different password for every account | Reuse a password, even with small changes |
| Store them in a password manager, or write them down and keep them somewhere safe | Leave written passwords where other people can see them |
| Turn on 2-step verification, starting with email | Assume a strong password alone is enough |
| Change a password straight away if it has been leaked | Change passwords on a schedule for no reason |
